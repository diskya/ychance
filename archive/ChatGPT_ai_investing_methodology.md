## Evidence base

There is strong support for the first version’s claim that markets underreact across **economic and informational links**. Customer-supplier relationships predict returns because investors do not instantly carry information from one linked firm to another. Shared analyst coverage also speeds or shapes diffusion, and price discovery often runs from prominent, highly followed firms toward neglected peers rather than the other way around. That means a serious AI methodology should maintain a live graph of supply chains, peer groups, analyst overlaps, and “bellwether-to-follower” links rather than treating each asset as an isolated time series. citeturn0search4turn1search0turn1search12turn19search1turn19search4

There is equally strong support for the idea that **attention constraints** are a core source of exploitable delay. Friday earnings announcements receive weaker immediate reactions and larger subsequent drift, which is classic evidence of limited attention. More recent work also shows that the intensity of contrarian retail trading around news and earnings helps explain momentum and post-earnings-announcement drift. In plain language, the market often misprices information not because it is secret, but because processing it is costly, poorly timed, or psychologically uncomfortable. That makes “attention mismatch” a materially better AI target than generic sentiment scoring. citeturn0search6turn0search18turn13search1turn13search11

The literature also strongly supports the claim that **flows and liquidity conditions** matter as much as informational correctness. Predictable uninformed cash flows, such as those tied to dividend-payment reinvestment or post-blackout-period stock selling, forecast returns, and these effects are larger when market liquidity is low. On the intermediation side, BIS and New York Fed work shows that dealer balance-sheet stress sharply worsens market liquidity, and BIS evidence in FX shows that the elasticity of liquidity provision collapses when dealer constraints bind. IMF work adds another useful nuance: the identity of the holder matters, because institutional ETF ownership can amplify volatility in the underlying bond market while retail ownership partly offsets that effect. citeturn12search0turn9search8turn9search11turn12search2turn12search6

The evidence also favors Claude’s emphasis on **beliefs, disagreement, and calibration**. NBER work on machine forecast disagreement shows that dispersion in modeled beliefs predicts returns and lines up with heterogeneous expectations. Separate recent work finds that investors, professionals, and analysts make predictable mistakes in expectations, especially in turbulent periods. And recent portfolio research argues that the standard sequence of “predict returns first, optimize second” is flawed because portfolio usefulness depends much more on whether forecasts are right for the assets that matter in the final portfolio than on average pointwise forecast accuracy. That is a major reason the first version is correct to reject monolithic return forecasting, but it is also why the Claude version is correct to insist on explicit consensus reconstruction and probability discipline. citeturn18search1turn20search6turn20search5turn14search1turn7search0turn7search16

The more unconventional parts of the first version also have more support than many investors realize. As entity["people","Robert Shiller","economist"] has argued, narratives can go viral and move spending and investing behavior, so narrative tracking is not pseudo-depth if done carefully. IMF work shows that geopolitical shocks transmit into asset prices through trade restrictions, financial restrictions, confidence, and uncertainty channels. The evidence on AI itself is mixed in exactly the way both drafts imply. LLMs do show promise in interpreting headlines and in extracting signal from standardized financial statements. The **first version** is stronger where it most matters: it identifies a set of mechanisms that can actually be tested and falsified. Its world-model plus propagation-graph design maps directly onto the literature on economic links, analyst-linked spillovers, limited attention, predictable price pressure, and deteriorating liquidity elasticity under dealer stress. It also avoids one of the biggest mistakes in AI-for-investing discussions, which is confusing impressive language output with an investable causal model. ## Recommended methodology

My recommendation is to use AI as a **market-update-function model**. The objective is not “predict next return.” The objective is “estimate how quickly and through which channels the market will revise belief and position after a change in the world.” For each event, the system should answer six questions: what changed in economics or policy; which assets and balance sheets are exposed; what consensus believed before the shock; who is likely to update late; who may be forced to trade; and how much intermediation capacity exists to absorb the flow. That framing is the strongest synthesis of the two drafts, and it sits directly on the best-supported parts of the literature. citeturn0search4turn13search1turn12search0turn9search8turn18search1turn14search1

Operationally, every candidate trade idea should clear a small number of gates. It should have **causal materiality**, meaning the shock affects cash flows, financing conditions, regulation, supply chains, or portfolio demand in a way that could matter. It should have a **consensus gap**, meaning the prior market belief was visibly different from the newly implied state. It should have **propagation asymmetry**, meaning at least one linked asset or peer is likely to update more slowly than the first node. It should have **attention cost**, meaning the trade requires cross-entity reasoning, contradiction of a prevailing story, or processing at a bad time. It should have a plausible **forced-flow path**, and it should occur in a **liquidity regime** where the same flow will actually move price. Finally, it should have a short, explicit **falsification clock**. If you cannot say what invalidates the idea and by when, it is probably not a short- or mid-term trade idea but an opinion. citeturn1search12turn12search0turn9search11turn20search6turn21search1

That structure naturally favors **relative-value expressions** over outright directional bets. If the transmission path is customer to supplier, bellwether to neglected peer, or one financing regime versus another, the clean expression is often a spread, basket, or hedge-adjusted pair rather than naked beta. The first version was right on this point. In practice, the most durable opportunities are often in second-order winners and losers: suppliers not yet repriced after a customer shock, neglected peers after a bellwether signal, duration-sensitive firms after a funding shock, or cross-market spillovers from geopolitical or stablecoin-driven dollar demand. citeturn0search4turn19search1turn17search2turn5search0turn5search1

Just as important is what to **de-emphasize**. Do not center the methodology on generic sentiment pipelines, monolithic “buy/sell” LLM prompts, off-the-shelf time-series foundation models, or autonomous multi-agent roleplay. Those approaches can be useful components, but they should not be the alpha thesis. The alpha thesis should remain: **identify where public information meets slow belief updating and constrained liquidity**. AI helps by converting unstructured evidence into economic primitives, propagating shocks through complex graphs, and enforcing disciplined decision rules. It should not be allowed to replace those primitives with eloquent but ungrounded narratives. citeturn6search1turn6search2turn16search0turn15search0

## Validation protocol

A robust research loop should begin with event parsing, not with market screens. The AI ingests filings, transcripts, regulatory documents, macro releases, policy changes, supply-chain data, and alternative data; converts them into structured economic objects; maps exposures across the graph; reconstructs the likely pre-event consensus; and then estimates where the combined attention and flow asymmetry is greatest. Only after that should it rank trade candidates. This preserves first-principles causality and uses AI where it is genuinely strong: text translation, cross-domain synthesis, and structured reasoning over many linked entities. citeturn6search0turn6search3turn0search4turn1search12

The validation standard should be much harsher than standard quant backtests. Tests must be walk-forward, point-in-time, cost-inclusive, and designed around **decision quality**, not just prediction error. That means measuring whether the trade chosen at time \(t\) after slippage, borrow, turnover, and capacity actually outperforms a baseline decision, not whether the model assigned slightly lower MSE to returns. It also means actively checking for the bias failures highlighted in recent finance-LLM work: look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, narrative bias, objective bias, and cost bias. If the strategy only works before costs, only works with revised data, or only works when the narrative is obvious in hindsight, it should be rejected. citeturn14search1turn7search16turn7search0turn6search2

The forecasting layer should be treated like a probabilistic discipline, not a storytelling contest. This is where Claude’s calibration instinct is genuinely valuable. Following the logic associated with entity["people","Philip Tetlock","forecasting researcher"], the system should log explicit probabilities, estimate calibration with Brier-style scoring, monitor whether 70% convictions are actually right about 70% of the time, and retrain or downweight modules that are systematically overconfident. P&L matters, but in the short run it is too noisy to be the only judge; calibration and falsification discipline tell you sooner whether the methodology is real or decorative. citeturn21search1turn21search7

## Strategic recommendation

The best place to start is **global equities**, specifically event-driven and relative-value research around earnings, supply-chain linkages, bellwether-follower relationships, and financing sensitivity. That universe has the richest public graph structure, the best studied attention anomalies, the cleanest point-in-time filings and transcript data, and the strongest academic replication of cross-firm information diffusion. It is where your methodology can be tested hardest and refined fastest without needing heroic assumptions. citeturn0search4turn1search0turn19search1turn13search1

The second expansion should be into **macro, rates, and FX**, but only after the core equity framework is working. That extension is attractive because liquidity elasticity, dealer balance-sheet capacity, geopolitical transmission, and stablecoin-driven dollar demand now have real documented effects in rates and FX markets. But it is also a harder domain operationally, because the exposure graph is less discrete and the transmission channels are more regime-dependent. For that reason, I would treat crypto less as the initial alpha field and more as a **cross-market spillover sensor**, especially for short-bill demand and FX stress transmission. citeturn9search8turn9search11turn17search2turn5search0turn5search1turn5search3

Use **the first version as the core methodology**, but add four things from the Claude version that materially improve it: explicit consensus mapping, outside-view base rates, adversarial red-teaming, and forecast calibration. If you do that, you get a methodology that is both empirically grounded and practically sharp.
